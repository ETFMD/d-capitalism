Infinite Buying Guidebook

From how 3× leveraged ETFs actually move, to the rules of Infinite Buying V4.0, the daily order routine and a real backtest on TQQQ and SOXL. Read from the top and you will be able to use the tracker above with confidence — even if you have never heard of the method before.

CreatorLaoer (라오어) — the Korean investor who created Infinite Buying (무한매수법) and keeps revising its rules. The official rules are published in Korean on Laoer’s Naver Cafe ↗ and in the book Laoer’s U.S. Stock Infinite Buying (Alki, 2021).

The 3-minute version — what is Infinite Buying?

  1. Split the moneyDivide your capital into 20, 30 or 40 portions and buy at most one portion a day.
  2. Buy at the closeUse limit-on-close (LOC) orders, so you only buy when the U.S. closing price is at or below your price.
  3. Sell on the bounceWhen the price rises a set % above your average cost (15% for TQQQ, 20% for SOXL), sell in parts and close the cycle.
  4. RepeatStart the next cycle with your capital plus the profit.

⚠ Infinite Buying does not remove losses. 3× leveraged ETFs have fallen about 80% in a single year, and you can lose money even when you follow every rule. This guide is educational, not investment advice. Infinite Buying is Laoer’s published method; this page is an independent explanation, not an official document. If the rules change, Laoer’s cafe announcements take precedence.

PART 1Leveraged ETF basics — know the product first

Infinite Buying is designed for 3× leveraged ETFs such as TQQQ and SOXL. Before memorising rules, understand how these products move differently from ordinary stocks — it makes the same drawdown far easier to sit through.

1-1How does a 3× leveraged ETF move?It targets 3× the daily return3 min

TQQQ (ProShares UltraPro QQQ) targets 3× the daily return of the Nasdaq-100, and SOXL (Direxion Daily Semiconductor Bull 3X) targets 3× the daily return of a U.S. semiconductor index. The fund does not simply buy three times the stocks: it uses swaps and futures to hold about 3× its net assets in index exposure, and resets that ratio near every close (daily rebalancing).

Key point “3×” applies to one day. If the index gains 20% in a year, a 3× ETF does not gain 60%. The longer the period, the further the result drifts from 3×.
Index daily move1× (e.g. QQQ)3× (e.g. TQQQ)
+2%+2%+6%
−2%−2%−6%
−10%−10%−30%
−33.4%−33.4%about −100% (effectively wiped out)

That is why leveraged ETFs carry costs and risks ordinary stocks don’t:

  • Expense ratio: around 1% a year (check the issuer’s site), deducted from the price daily.
  • Financing cost: the higher interest rates are, the more it costs to maintain leverage.
  • Volatility decay: losses build up even when the index just goes up and down (next chapter).

In one line — a 3× ETF is not “a stock that rises 3×”; it is “a product reset to 3× every day”.

1-2Volatility decay — losing money while the index goes nowhereThe bigger the swings, the bigger the drag4 min

If an index rises 10% and then falls 9.09%, it is exactly back where it started (100 → 110 → 100). Over the same two days a 3× ETF goes 100 → 130 → 94.5 — a 5.5% loss, because the second day’s 3× move is applied to a bigger base. This is volatility decay.

In a steady trend it works the other way: ten straight days of +1% give the index +10.5% and a 3× ETF +34.4% (more than 3 × 10.5 = 31.5%); ten straight days of −1% give −9.6% and −26.3% (less than 3 × −9.6 = −28.7%).

Why it matters for Infinite Buying Simply holding a 3× ETF melts it down in a choppy market. Infinite Buying buys in small parts on the way down to lower the average cost, then takes only the target profit on a rebound — an attempt to sidestep this drag. A long decline or a long sideways market can still produce losses.

In one line — 3× ETFs love clear trends and hate back-and-forth markets.

U.S. daylight saving time runs from the second Sunday of March to the first Sunday of November (in 2026: March 8 – November 1). There are no fills on U.S. market holidays. Korean brokers’ “daytime trading” of U.S. stocks is not used in Infinite Buying.

Infinite Buying trades once a day, based on the regular-session close. That is why it relies on LOC orders (buy at the close if the close is at or below your price; sell at the close if it is at or above). The closing price is set the moment the regular session ends, and only LOC orders that meet their condition fill.

When to place orders Place the target-% limit sell when the pre-market opens and keep it live through after-hours; place LOC orders any time before the regular close. Brokers stop accepting LOC orders a few to fifteen minutes before the close, so entering them early is safest.

In one line — place orders before the close, check fills after it.

1-7Currency, fees and taxesYour real return is not just the dollar return3 min

Currency: if your home currency is not the U.S. dollar, exchange-rate moves change your result. Make 10% in dollars while the dollar falls 10% against your currency and you end up roughly flat (1.10 × 0.90 = 0.99); a rising dollar does the opposite.

Fees: many U.S. brokers charge no commission, but brokers in other countries often charge around 0.1–0.25% per trade plus a currency-conversion spread. Infinite Buying trades often, so small fees add up — compare before opening an account.

Taxes: rules depend on where you live. For example, Korean residents pay 22% (including local tax) on annual net gains from foreign stocks above ₩2.5 million, filed the following May, with same-year losses netted against gains. Check your own country’s capital-gains rules.

PART 2Infinite Buying in practice — rules and daily routine

This part explains the V4.0 rules exactly the way the tracker above calculates them. You don’t need to memorise formulas — record your fills and the tracker builds each day’s order sheet. Knowing why each order appears makes it much easier to stick to the rules in a crash.

2-1Infinite Buying at a glanceOne cycle from start to finish3 min
  1. Set up Choose the ticker (TQQQ or SOXL), the number of splits (Laoer recommends 20, 30 or 40) and your capital.
  2. First buy On day one, place one portion as an LOC order 10–15% above the previous close (the tracker’s default is 12%) — the “big-number” price, which in practice fills at the close — plus a few lower LOC lines.
  3. Buy in parts every day When the price is near your average cost or the star price, buy one portion (or half). Each purchase raises the T value (portions bought).
  4. Sell in parts If the close is at or above the star price, sell ¼ of your shares (quarter sell); if the price reaches your average cost + target % (15% TQQQ, 20% SOXL), the limit order sells the rest.
  5. Close the cycle When you hold zero shares the cycle ends; start the next one with the profit included.
  6. If the money runs out When T exceeds splits − 1 and not even one portion is left (exhausted), switch to reverse mode and hold on by selling small amounts of your shares.
What the tracker does Record your trades and it calculates the T value, average cost, star price and daily buy amount, then builds today’s order sheet (LOC buys, quarter sell, limit sell). Sign in and your records are saved to your account, so you can continue on any phone or PC.

In one line — buy in parts, sell in parts once the target is hit, repeat.

2-2Plan your capital before you startCapital comes before rules3 min
  • Only use money you can afford to see fall 80% without it affecting your life — revisit chapter 1-3 first.
  • Daily buy amount = capital ÷ number of splits. $20,000 in 40 splits is $500 a portion. A portion should be several times the share price (at least 3–5×) so you can buy every day.
  • Keep separate capital per ticker. To run TQQQ and SOXL together, create two sessions in the tracker, each with its own capital.
  • Keep enough dollars ready. You buy almost every day; if you are short on cash, that day’s order is missed.
  • Don’t add or withdraw money mid-cycle. If you must, record it with the “capital” change in the menu so the daily amount is recalculated.

2-6First half, second half and the big-number buyWhere the buying changes3 min
  • First half (T < half the splits): split the daily amount in two — half at star price − 1 cent, half at the average cost, both LOC. You buy a full portion only when the price falls near the average; on a slightly higher close you buy half.
  • Second half (T ≥ half the splits): star % drops below 0%, so the star price sits below the average. The whole daily amount goes to star price − 1 cent — spending what is left more carefully.
  • Big-number price: if the star price is far above the previous close (above previous close × 1.12), brokers may reject an order that far from the market (about 20% away in Laoer’s notes), so the buy is placed at the “big number” (previous close × 1.12, default 12%). All order-sheet lines priced at or above the big number are combined into one line at the big-number price, and the lines below continue one share each. Example: with a 12-share star buy, a 13-share average buy and 1-share ladder lines, a big number of $33.33 places 30 shares at $33.33, then 1 share each at $32.25, $31.25 … Because LOC fills at the close, the result is the same as the original sheet unless the stock jumps more than 12% in a day.
  • Adjust the big-number %: Laoer treats +5% to +20% above the previous close as a guide mid-cycle. What matters is buying for sure below the star price: raise it above 20% after a big gap up, lower it to 5–10% after a gap down. It is an order-entry device, not part of the method itself; change it or turn it off in the menu.
  • Rejected sell orders: in a deep decline the quarter sell or limit sell can be too far from the market and get rejected. Laoer’s rule is simply not to place it that day — never move the sell price to make it fit.

2-7Quarter sells and closing a cycleWhy sell in parts2 min

There are two sell orders. The quarter sell sells ¼ of your shares LOC when the close is at or above the star price, recovering some cash (T × 0.75). The limit sell places the other ¾ at average cost + target % (15% TQQQ, 20% SOXL). If only the limit fills, ¼ remains and T becomes T × 0.25; if the quarter sell fills the same day too, you hold zero and the cycle ends. If the limit fills and the price then drops enough for an LOC buy to fill as well, the cycle continues with T × 0.25 + 1 (+0.5 for a half buy).

In the second half the star price is below the average, so a quarter sell can lock in a loss. That is not a mistake — it rebuilds cash before the capital runs out.

When a cycle closes the tracker shows the realised profit with a celebration screen and archives the cycle in the “analysis” tab. Start the next cycle with the same capital or with the result included.

In one line — sell ¼ often to keep breathing room, sell ¾ at the target in one go.

2-8When the money runs out — exhausted and reverse modeRules for long declines4 min

If the price keeps falling and T exceeds splits − 1, less than one portion of cash remains. That is exhaustion; under Laoer’s rules you switch to reverse mode from that point even if some cash remains, and use that cash in reverse mode. The tracker flags the exhausted state and shows a button to switch.

  1. Day one Sell shares ÷ (splits ÷ 2) MOC. Example: 400 shares in 20 splits → 40 shares.
  2. From day two Use the average of the previous five closes as the “reverse star price” and place two orders: ① an LOC sell of that day’s shares ÷ (splits ÷ 2) (rounded down) at the reverse star price, and ② an LOC quarter buy with ¼ of remaining cash (including what you sold) at reverse star price − 1 cent. Depending on the close, one of them fills. In reverse mode, exhaustion is redefined as the day a quarter buy can no longer buy even one share; that day you sell MOC as on day one.
  3. T value: a reverse sell multiplies T by 0.9 (20 splits) or 0.95 (40 splits); a quarter buy makes it T + (splits − T) × 0.25. Laoer specifies only 20 and 40 splits; for 30 the tracker uses × (1 − 2 ÷ splits).
  4. Back to normal When the close rises above average cost × (1 − target %) (TQQQ: above −15%, SOXL: above −20%), normal mode resumes the next day. T carries over and the daily amount is recalculated as cash ÷ (splits − T); if there is still not a full portion, reverse mode starts again. The tracker shows the return price.
What reverse mode means It sells part of your shares on each bounce to raise cash, then buys back lower to pull the average down. The longer the recovery takes, the more losses you lock in — the best protection is choosing enough splits and capital that you rarely get here.

In one line — when cash runs out, switch to selling-while-buying to lower the average and hold on.

2-9Ten common beginner mistakesA final check before your first order3 min
  1. Missing the LOC cut-off — check your broker’s deadline and enter orders early.
  2. Getting daylight saving wrong — outside U.S. daylight time the session opens one hour later in Asian and European time zones.
  3. Recording late — a few missed days throw off T and the average, and the order sheet goes wrong. Record after every close.
  4. Recording the order sheet instead of the fill — record partial fills with the actual shares.
  5. Placing LOC orders as plain limit orders — they fill intraday and break the close-based rules. Always choose LOC.
  6. Stopping in a crash — skipping the cheapest days stops the average from falling. If the capital was too much to bear, it should have been smaller from the start.
  7. Cancelling the limit sell out of greed — miss the target exit and you are back in the decay zone.
  8. Mixing tickers in one pot — keep a separate session and capital per ticker.
  9. Running short of dollars — no cash on a buy day means the whole order is missed.
  10. Forgetting taxes and currency — your real return depends on both (chapter 1-7).

In one line — most failures come from order mistakes and breaking the rules, not from the math.

3-5How far can you trust a backtest?Reading past returns3 min
  • Period bias — U.S. tech since 2010 has been a historically unusual bull market. Judged on this period alone, almost anything with a 3× ETF looks good.
  • Fill assumptions — backtests assume your full size fills at the close. In reality there are cut-offs, partial fills, holidays and your own mistakes.
  • Missing costs — fees, currency spreads and taxes can cut several points a year.
  • Overfitting — the splits and target % that fit the past best are likely to fit only the past.
  • Human limits — placing orders every day through a −80% drawdown is far harder than the math.

In one line — a backtest shows what was possible, not what you will earn.

How the Infinite Buying order sheet is calculated

Infinite Buying (무한매수법) is a rule-based way to trade 3× leveraged ETFs such as TQQQ and SOXL, created by the Korean investor Laoer: split your capital into 20–40 portions, buy a little every day with limit-on-close orders, and sell in parts once the price is a set % above your average cost. Record your fills and this tracker calculates the T value, average cost and star price, and builds the order sheet to place today.

How it works

  • Daily buy amount = remaining cash ÷ (splits − T)
  • T value: full buy +1, half buy +0.5, quarter sell ×0.75, target-price sell ×0.25 — how many portions you have bought.
  • Star % = target − target × 2 ÷ splits × T (default target: TQQQ 15%, SOXL 20%)
  • Star price = average cost × (1 + star %) — buy one tick below it, quarter-sell LOC at it.
  • First half (T < half the splits): half the daily amount at the star price, half at the average cost (LOC) · second half: all at the star price
  • Sells: ¼ of the shares LOC at the star price, the other ¾ as a limit order at average × (1 + target %)
  • Exhausted → reverse mode: once T exceeds splits − 1, the order sheet switches to Laoer’s reverse-mode rules.

FAQ

Who created Infinite Buying?

Infinite Buying (무한매수법) was created by Laoer (라오어, a pen name). The official rules and revisions are published in Korean on Laoer’s Naver Cafe and in the book Laoer’s U.S. Stock Infinite Buying (Alki, 2021). This page is an independent tool that calculates and records orders with the V4.0 rules; if the rules change, the cafe announcements take precedence.

What is an LOC order?

A limit-on-close (LOC) order fills at the closing price if the close is at or below (buy) / at or above (sell) your price. Infinite Buying trades once a day on the close, so it relies on LOC orders offered by U.S. brokers.

Why is the T value a decimal?

A half fill adds only 0.5, and a quarter sell multiplies T by 0.75. A low T means a high star %, so buy and sell thresholds are higher; a high T (more bought) lowers them so you buy cheaper and exit sooner.

Did Infinite Buying beat buy and hold in the past?

Not on return. In the real-data backtest in chapter 3-6 (TQQQ and SOXL since 2010, 27 settings, recalculated daily) the default settings earned less per year than simply holding, but with much shallower maximum drawdowns. See the live table above for current numbers.

Where are my records stored?

If you sign in, all records are saved to your account automatically and follow you to any phone or PC. Without signing in they stay only in this browser (localStorage) and are uploaded to your account when you sign in later.

Can Infinite Buying lose money?

Yes. TQQQ and SOXL track 3× the daily return, so a long decline can shrink your capital sharply, and if the market does not recover after the cash is used up, losses are realised. This page helps you calculate and record orders under the method; investment decisions are your own responsibility.
